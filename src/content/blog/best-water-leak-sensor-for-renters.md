The best water leak sensor for renters is a small, battery or plug-in device that sits on the floor, sends a phone alert the moment it gets wet, and goes back in the box on move-out day. A water leak sensor (also called a smart water leak detector or flood sensor) uses metal probes or a sensing cable to detect water, then sounds an alarm or pushes a notification through an app. I picked three that need no drilling, no plumbing and no landlord tools, and I explain where to put them, what your lease likely says about leaks, and how to document water damage.

## Short answer: the best water leak sensor for renters

**For most renters, the YoLink Water Leak Sensor 1 kit is the pick for big units or weak Wi-Fi, the Eve Water Guard is the pick for Apple Home, and the Flo by Moen detector is the pick for one hub-free Wi-Fi sensor.** None of them touches your pipes, and all three leave the apartment exactly as you found it.

Here is how I would match them to your place:

- **Basement unit, far-off laundry or spotty Wi-Fi:** the YoLink kit, because its LoRa radio reaches farther than Wi-Fi.
- **iPhone household:** the Eve Water Guard, whose 6.5 ft sensing cable covers a whole run of floor.
- **One sensor, no hub, extra readings:** the Flo by Moen Smart Water Detector, which also tracks temperature and humidity.

## How we chose a water leak sensor for renters

**A water leak sensor for renters made our list only if it needs no drilling or plumbing, sends remote alerts with no required subscription mentioned in our sources, and has its own clear Amazon US listing.** That last rule matters because a link to a search page is no use to you, and it trimmed the list to three.

My renter checklist looked like this:

1. **No-drill placement.** The sensor lies on the floor or plugs in; nothing is screwed or glued.
2. **No subscription.** None of our sources mention a required subscription for alerts on these three.
3. **Alerts that survive a weak signal.** A Wi-Fi sensor under a sink, behind a fridge or in a closet can lose its link, so range matters.
4. **Honest owner feedback.** I read owner reviews and forum posts online, not just spec sheets. The numbers are small (six owner sources for YoLink, two for Eve, three for Flo), so I treat them as patterns, not proof.
5. **Clean move-out.** Everything has to come out in minutes.

We left both conflicts in this guide instead of picking the friendlier number. The owner threads added a third pattern: setup friction came up more often than missed leaks.

## Water leak detector for apartments: the 3 picks reviewed

**A water leak detector for apartments has to fit your layout: each pick below suits a different apartment, so pick by your Wi-Fi coverage and the phone or smart home app you already use.** I base each review on manufacturer specs, professional reviews and owner reports I read online; I did not test these.

### YoLink Water Leak Sensor 1 Kit (4 sensors + hub): Best for big or thick-walled apartments

The [YoLink Water Leak Sensor 1 kit](amzn:YoLink+Water+Leak+Sensor+1+Kit+4-Pack+Hub+YS7903+LoRa) pairs a hub with four Water Leak Sensor 1 units (YS7903). The sensors talk to the hub over LoRa, a long-range, low-power radio that typically gets through walls and floors better than Wi-Fi, and the hub connects to your router over Ethernet or 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi. Reviews rate the hub at up to about a quarter mile in open air, though range figures differ between sources, so treat that as a maximum. Four sensors in one box lets you cover the kitchen sink, bathroom, water heater and washer at once.

Some forum owners describe the system as dependable and say the range reaches basements and far rooms; we could not independently confirm those posts, so treat them as anecdotes. The main complaint, from both owners and reviewers, is setup: you need the hub and the YoLink app, and linking automations by hand feels cumbersome. Owners in smart home forum threads also report sensors dropping offline or out of third-party integrations until they re-pair them or power-cycle the hub. In setups where a YoLink valve shuts the water off automatically, some owners say sensors must be lifted before mopping so wet floors do not trigger the valve; that does not apply to the sensors on their own.

### Eve Water Guard: Best for Apple Home

The [Eve Water Guard](amzn:Eve+Water+Guard+connected+water+leak+detector+HomeKit+Thread) is a plug-in base with a 6.5 ft (2 m) sensing cable that detects water anywhere along its length, plus a 100 dB alarm. That cable is the real difference: instead of guarding one spot, you can run it along the front of a dishwasher, behind a fridge water line or around a water heater. Extensions are sold separately. It connects over Thread, a low-power mesh network, and Bluetooth, so there is no bridge and no cloud account; remote alerts and automations need an Apple home hub.

Smart Home Compared describes owners as generally pleased with its reliability and HomeKit integration, and the long cable is the feature reviewers keep returning to. The trade-offs are plain: it is Apple Home only, with no Alexa, Google or SmartThings support, and it needs constant wall power, so the outlet decides where it can go. A small share of buyers also report connection failures, per the reviews we read. TechHive lists the lack of Android support as a drawback, so an Android household should skip it.

### Flo by Moen Smart Water Detector: Best app and monitoring

The [Flo by Moen Smart Water Detector](amzn:Flo+by+Moen+Smart+Water+Detector) is a single Wi-Fi puck (model 920-004, sold as a 1-pack) that joins your network directly, with no hub. Besides water, it reads temperature and humidity, and Reviewed says alerts reach iOS, Android and email quickly. This is the stand-alone detector, not Moen's whole-home Flo shutoff valve. That valve is plumbed into the main water line, which makes it a landlord decision, not a renter purchase.

Review summaries of this detector, such as Looria and Kimola, point to reliable leak detection once it is set up and appreciation for the extra temperature and humidity readings, though we could not verify every point on those pages. The complaints noted are the higher cost per sensor and some reports of dead batteries on arrival; reviews of Moen's wider Flo line also mention false alarms and weak support, though those reports are not always about this detector. TechHive and Reviewed both note there is no smart assistant or IFTTT integration. Sources also disagree on the battery type, so check the box.

## Smart water leak detector buying guide for apartments

**A smart water leak detector should be chosen by how it connects first, then by alarm, power and app.** Connection decides whether the water alert ever reaches your phone as a push notification.

### Wi-Fi vs hub vs Thread: how a water sensor reaches your phone

A water sensor on Wi-Fi, like the Flo, joins your router directly, which is simple but depends on a strong signal at floor level. A hub-based sensor like YoLink uses its own radio to reach the hub, which helps in large or concrete-walled units. A Thread sensor like the Eve builds a mesh with other Thread devices and leans on an Apple home hub for remote alerts. If your router sits at the far end of the apartment, I would lean toward a model with a hub required, since the hub talks to Wi-Fi so the sensor does not have to. Zigbee is another low-power mesh protocol many leak sensors use, and Matter is the cross-platform smart home standard; our sources did not list Zigbee or Matter support for these three picks.

### Leak alarm, battery and smart home fit

Weigh these points before you buy:

- **Loud alarm:** the Eve's water leak alarm is a 100 dB siren; our sources did not confirm how loud the alarm is on the other two.
- **Power:** a battery-powered sensor goes anywhere, and the battery is replaceable on the YoLink; a plug-in sensor needs an outlet within reach.
- **Ecosystem:** Apple Home for Eve, YoLink's own app for YoLink, Moen's app for Flo. If you are still choosing a platform, our [smart home for renters guide](/blog/smart-home-for-renters/) walks through the options.

### Flood sensor vs shutoff valve

A water leak sensor only warns you; a shutoff valve actually stops the water. Whole-home shutoff valves, including Moen's, are plumbed into the supply line, so a renter should not install one. If you want automatic shutoff, ask your landlord in writing; otherwise your shutoff valve is the one under the sink or behind the toilet, so learn where it is on day one.

## Where to put a water sensor in a rental

**A water sensor works best flat on the floor wherever water lines or drains meet an appliance, starting with the spots you cannot see.** Water runs to the lowest point, so place the probes there.

1. **Under the kitchen and bathroom sinks**, at the back of the under-sink cabinet near the supply lines.
2. **Behind the toilet**, near the fill valve hose.
3. **Next to the water heater**, if your unit has one.
4. **Beside the washing machine and dishwasher**, where a hose failure shows up first.
5. **Behind the refrigerator**, if it has an ice maker or water line.
6. **Near shared walls or under a ceiling stain**, if a neighbor lives above or beside you.

Smoke and water are the two alarms I want every renter to have; if you are adding phone alerts for fire too, see our [smart smoke detectors for apartments](/blog/best-smart-smoke-detector-for-apartments/) guide.

## Leaks, your lease and renters insurance

**A water leak sensor for renters gives you a timestamped alert, which helps because many leases ask renters to report leaks promptly.** Who pays for the damage depends on your lease, the cause and your insurance, so check your lease and local law; this is not legal advice.

### What to tell your landlord and when

Look for a landlord notification clause in your lease: it usually says how fast renters must tell the landlord about a leak and how. When a sensor alerts, stop the water if you safely can, then message your landlord or super in writing right away. Here is a message I would send:

> Hi [name], my water sensor alerted at [time] on [date]. There is water under the [kitchen sink / water heater / toilet] in unit [number]. I turned off the valve under the sink and put down towels. Photos attached. Can someone check it today? Thanks, [your name].

### Documenting a leak for a claim

Save the app's alert history, take photos and a short video before you clean up, and keep a copy of the message you sent your landlord. Typically, a landlord's own insurance covers the building, while your renters insurance covers your belongings, but your policy is what decides that.

### Possible insurance discounts

A leak sensor may or may not lower your renters insurance; some renters ask their insurer whether it earns a discount. We have not seen a source that confirms one for these products, so ask your agent directly.

## Moving out: taking your water sensor with you

**A water sensor that stands on the floor is fully portable: unplug it, pick it up and remove it from the app.** Every sensor you move out with should leave no marks behind. On move-out day, I would do this:

1. Remove each sensor and the hub or base, and coil any sensing cable.
2. Delete the devices from the app, or reset them, so the next tenant can start fresh.
3. If you used removable adhesive to route a hub cord, peel strips slowly and straight down; our [adhesive hooks for renters](/blog/best-heavy-duty-adhesive-hooks-for-renters/) guide covers damage-free removal.
4. Photograph the under-sink cabinets and floors to show they are dry and clean.

If you travel often, pair a sensor with a camera so you can see what an alert means; our [wireless security cameras for apartments](/blog/best-wireless-security-cameras-for-apartments/) guide lists renter-safe options. And give a neighbor or friend a spare key so someone can stop a leak when you cannot.
