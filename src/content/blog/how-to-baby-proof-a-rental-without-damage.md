Learning how to baby proof a rental without damage comes down to one rule I repeat to every renter friend: clamp, plug, snap or stick wherever you can, and screw into the wall only where safety demands it. Baby proofing a rental is the same job as baby proofing any home (block stairs, lock cabinets, cover outlets, stop furniture from tipping), with one extra goal: getting your full security deposit back. In this renter baby proofing guide I'll walk you through what needs landlord permission, a room-by-room plan, a landlord email you can copy, move-out day, and six tools that do the work without drilling.

## Can you baby proof a rental without damage?

**Yes, you can baby proof most of a rental without damage, because the core gear now comes in pressure-mounted, plug-in, snap-on and adhesive versions.** The one honest exception is anchoring tall furniture, which needs a single screw into a wall stud.

Here is the short version of what baby proofing an apartment involves and how each fix attaches:

- **Stairs and doorways:** a pressure-mounted baby gate, which tensions against the door frame instead of screwing into it (doorways, hallways and the bottom of stairs only).
- **Cabinets and drawers:** an adhesive cabinet lock or magnetic cabinet lock, a hidden latch inside the door that releases only when you hold a magnetic key on the outside.
- **Outlets:** an outlet cover, usually a plug-in cap that pushes into an unused socket.
- **Sharp edges:** a corner guard, a foam or silicone cushion for coffee tables and TV stands.
- **Doors:** snap-on door knob covers that keep toddlers from turning round knobs.
- **Tall furniture:** a furniture anti-tip strap, which addresses the tip-over hazard of dressers, TVs and bookcases and needs one stud screw and your landlord's OK.
- **Blind cords:** a cord shortener that keeps looped cords out of reach.

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) keeps a plain-language set of [child safety guides for kids and babies](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/Kids-and-Babies), and I'd read it alongside this guide. Rental rules change how you attach things; they never change what needs protecting.

## What baby proofing needs landlord permission?

**Anything that puts a screw into a wall, door frame or window needs written landlord permission; anything that clamps, plugs in or snaps on usually doesn't.** In practice that means anti-tip straps, hardware-mounted stair gates and window guards are the conversations to have.

### Check your lease first

Your lease is the rulebook for baby proofing a rental, so read it before you buy anything. I look for four clauses:

1. **Alterations or improvements:** often bans "holes, nails or fixtures" without written consent.
2. **Wall fixtures or decorations:** sometimes allows small nails but not anchors.
3. **Security deposit deductions:** lists what the landlord can charge for at move-out.
4. **Normal wear and tear:** the everyday aging a landlord generally cannot deduct for; a patched, painted screw hole is usually treated differently from a ragged one.

This isn't legal advice, and tenant law varies by state and city, so check your lease and local law if anything is unclear.

### How to ask your landlord

The easiest yes comes from a short, specific request that promises to restore the wall. Here's the email I'd send, ready to copy:

> Subject: Request to anchor furniture for child safety, Unit [#]
>
> Hi [Name], we have a baby on the way (or: a toddler who is starting to climb) and want to follow child safety guidance by anchoring our tall dresser and bookcase to the wall so they can't tip over. Each anchor uses one small screw into a wall stud. When we move out, we'll remove the screws, fill the holes with spackle and touch up the paint. We'd also like to ask about a hardware-mounted gate at the top of the stairs. Could you confirm in writing that this is OK? Happy to use a specific paint or contractor if you prefer. Thanks, [Your name]

Save the reply in the same folder as your lease; some landlords will turn it into a lease addendum, which is even better written approval. If a landlord says no to anchors, ask what they will allow, and keep tall furniture out of the baby's rooms until you agree on something.

## How to baby proof an apartment room by room

**Start with the hazards a crawling baby reaches first: stairs, outlets, tip-prone furniture and low cabinets, then work room by room.** Before any of it, photograph every wall, cabinet face, door frame and outlet you'll touch, with the date visible in your phone's photo info.

### Living room

The living room is where a crawling baby spends the most floor time. Plug every unused outlet, pad the coffee table and TV stand corners, and anchor the TV stand or bookcase if it's tall or top-heavy. Get cords off the floor; I like routing them up and out of reach with damage-free hooks from our [heavy-duty adhesive hooks guide](/blog/best-heavy-duty-adhesive-hooks-for-renters/), mounted high on furniture backs or painted walls you've cleaned first.

### Kitchen

The kitchen is where adhesive cabinet locks earn their keep. Lock the cabinet with cleaning products first, then knives, then anything glass. Mount locks inside the doors so any adhesive mark is hidden, and use a pressure-mounted gate in the kitchen doorway if your layout allows it.

### Bathroom

The bathroom door is the simplest fix: a door knob cover keeps a toddler from getting in at all. Inside, lock the vanity cabinet and plug any unused outlets near the sink.

### Nursery and bedroom

The nursery is where tip-over hazards matter most, because dressers and bookcases are tall and drawers make tempting steps. Anchor them, keep the crib clear of window cords, and cover outlets behind the furniture too.

### Stairs, windows and doors

Every stair gate gets one of two answers: a pressure-mounted gate at the bottom is fine, but the top needs a hardware-mounted gate. Windows need cord shorteners for blinds and a window stop that limits how far the sash opens; ask your landlord before installing anything that screws into the window frame. Door knob covers can keep a toddler out of closets and bathrooms.

## How to choose gear for baby proofing a rental

**Baby proofing a rental works best when you choose gear by how it attaches, how cleanly it comes off, and whether it actually solves the hazard in that spot.** A pretty adhesive gadget that falls off in a week is worse than one small, patched screw hole.

This table compares how each type of baby proofing gear attaches and what it leaves behind:

| Gear | How it attaches | Landlord permission? | What it leaves behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure-mounted gate | Tension against the frame, wall cups | Usually no | Possible scuffs from wall cups |
| Magnetic cabinet lock | Adhesive inside the cabinet (screws on particle board) | Usually no for adhesive | Hidden adhesive marks |
| Plug-in outlet covers | Push into unused outlets | No | Nothing |
| Foam corner guards | Adhesive strip on furniture | No (on your own furniture) | Possible residue on some finishes |
| Door knob covers | Snap over a round knob | No | Nothing |
| Anti-tip strap | Stud screw plus adhesive pad or screw on furniture | Yes, ask first | One small hole to patch |

When I'm comparing gear, I also look for JPMA certification on gates, a clear statement on what surfaces the adhesive suits, and whether I can reuse the product in the next home.

## Baby proofing a rental with adhesive, without ruining paint

**Clean the surface, let it dry, press firmly and wait before loading the product; most adhesive damage comes from rushed installs and rushed removals.** Painted drywall is the riskiest surface, so keep adhesive on cabinet interiors and your own furniture wherever you can.

These steps protect your deposit:

1. Photograph the spot before installing.
2. Wipe the surface with rubbing alcohol and let it dry completely.
3. Press the adhesive firmly for the time the instructions give, then wait before using the lock or guard.
4. On removal, warm the pad briefly with a hair dryer on its lowest setting, then work dental floss behind it in a sawing motion instead of pulling straight off.
5. Clean any leftover residue with a residue remover that's safe for the finish, testing a hidden spot first.

## When baby proofing a rental needs more than no-drill

**No-drill gear isn't safe enough at the top of stairs or for tall, top-heavy furniture, and in both cases the right answer is a landlord conversation, not a workaround.** Never use a pressure-mounted gate at the top of stairs: a toddler pushing on it can knock it loose, and the fall that follows is exactly what the gate was meant to stop.

For tall furniture, an adhesive-only anchor isn't something I'd rely on. If your landlord refuses a stud screw, move tall dressers and bookcases out of the rooms your child uses, keep heavy items in bottom drawers, and never put a TV on top of a dresser. For the top of stairs, ask whether the landlord will approve a hardware-mounted gate, or install one themselves, and offer to patch the holes when you leave.

## Baby proofing a rental on move-out day

**Baby proofing a rental ends on move-out day: remove everything gently, patch any screw holes, clean adhesive residue, and photograph the result next to your original photos.** Most baby proofing gear goes straight into a box for the next home.

Here's the move-out order I'd follow:

1. Pull the outlet covers and door knob covers first; they leave nothing behind.
2. Loosen and remove the pressure-mounted gate, then wipe the frame where the wall cups sat.
3. Remove adhesive cabinet locks and corner guards with heat and floss, then clean residue.
4. Unscrew anti-tip straps, fill the stud hole with spackle, sand smooth and touch up the paint.
5. Retake the "before" photos from the same angles and save both sets with your lease.
